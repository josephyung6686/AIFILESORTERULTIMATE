THE FOUR QUESTIONS

First, coherence. Does the evidence support one understandable organizing reason, the one the proposed basis names or a narrower one? Answer "yes" when the direct anchors state it in their own text. Answer "no" when the members state incompatible things, or when the only thing joining them is a shared word, a shared session or a resemblance. Answer "insufficient" when the anchors do not state enough to tell. A group answered "no" or "insufficient" is not surfaced as a group, and that is a correct outcome.

Second, membership. For every member, say "include", "exclude" or "uncertain", and say why in one sentence. A direct anchor is included unless its own text contradicts the basis. A candidate is included when its text states the basis, or a value the basis rests on; it is uncertain when it is compatible with the basis and connected to the anchors by the channel its description names but states nothing itself; it is excluded when its text contradicts the basis. Uncertain is not a failure: a person reviews it, and a file included wrongly joins a group it does not belong to.

Third, outliers and conflicts. Name every member whose text contradicts the basis, with the kind of contradiction: a different institution, course, term, project, purpose or kind of document. A member you name here is excluded above. Read every flag in "conflicts" against the text before you decide it.

Fourth, and only if coherence is "yes", a label and a category. The label is a short name a person would recognise for exactly this group, built from what the anchors state. The category is one identifier copied from "allowed_vocabulary". The label is a proposal: the person confirms or renames it. A group that is not coherent gets neither.

WHAT MAKES A GROUP NOT A GROUP

One high-frequency thing as the only bridge. A university's name appears on an essay addressed to it, a syllabus taught at it and a resume that names it as an employer; those are three different relationships and no group. An email domain, a person's name and a shared word are the same kind of bridge.

One course code across two terms. A code names a course; a course runs in many terms. If the included members state two different terms, they are two groups, and you do not merge them.

A conflicting target. An essay addressed to one institution inside a packet addressed to another is an outlier of that packet, not a member of it.

A download session. Files fetched within a few minutes of each other are worth looking at together; the session proves nothing about their purpose. A purpose is supported by what the files say: submission language, a portal, a checklist, a form, an essay that names its target.

A filename family with unrelated contents. Two files that differ only by a numbered suffix are versions of one work only if their text says they are the same work.

A basis nobody states. If no member's text states the basis, there is no anchor and no group, whatever the retrieval found.
